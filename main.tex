\begin{abstract}
The emergence of reasoning models and inference-time scaling has altered how large language models process knowledge. While previous literature relied on static pipelines (retrieval-augmented generation, knowledge editing, continual learning), modern systems resolve conflicts during test-time compute. This shift exposes a methodological gap: the research community lacks a unified formalism for how knowledge persists, evolves, and is computed over across a model's lifespan. This paper introduces the \textbf{Knowledge Lifecycle}, a theoretical framework that organises LLM knowledge management into five structural stages: \textit{Acquisition}, \textit{Storage}, \textit{Retrieval}, \textit{Update}, and \textit{Forgetting}. We make two primary contributions. First, we propose the \textbf{Provenance Vector ($p$)}, an architectural modification that appends temporal metadata to parametric storage, serving as a reward signal for test-time conflict resolution and Representation Engineering. Second, we introduce \textbf{Lifecycle Desynchronisation ($\mathcal{D}_{sync}$)}, a formal mathematical metric based on KL-divergence designed to quantify the predictive divergence that occurs when parametric weights and non-parametric context conflict. Our analysis demonstrates that static updates are insufficient for modern architectures, and provides a formal roadmap for lifecycle-aware reasoning models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

Large language models serve as the backbone of systems that answer medical questions, draft legal briefs, write production code, and tutor students in mathematics~\citep{brown2020language, achiam2023gpt4, team2023gemini}. With the advent of inference-time scaling and reasoning models (e.g., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1), these systems no longer simply retrieve and generate; they execute extended test-time compute to search, reflect, and self-correct. However, this advanced reasoning is bound by a specific bottleneck: the model's underlying relationship with facts. A medical reasoning agent that computes over a retracted drug interaction, or a coding agent that builds logic around a deprecated API, represents a failure of knowledge management rather than a failure of reasoning capacity.

The research community has responded to these challenges, but it has done so in silos. General surveys on LLMs~\citep{zhao2023llm_survey, wang2024llm_agents_survey} touch on knowledge tangentially, while specific subfields operate in isolation. At least five separate subfields now address different pieces of the knowledge problem:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)} focuses on how to fetch relevant documents and feed them to the model at inference time~\citep{lewis2020rag, guu2020realm, gao2023rag_survey}.
  \item \textbf{Knowledge Editing} asks whether individual facts inside a model's weights can be surgically modified without retraining~\citep{meng2022locating, meng2023memit, yao2023editing_survey}.
  \item \textbf{Continual Learning} tackles how a model can absorb new information over time without destroying what it already knows~\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc, ke2023continual, wu2024continual_survey}.
  \item \textbf{Context Engineering} studies how to arrange, compress, and prioritise information within a finite context window~\citep{liu2024lost}.
  \item \textbf{Agent Memory} builds persistent storage that lets LLM-based agents remember and reuse past experience~\citep{park2023generative, packer2023memgpt, zhang2024survey_agent_memory}.
\end{enumerate}

Each subfield has its own dedicated surveys~\citep{gao2023rag_survey, mialon2023augmented, zhang2024survey_agent_memory, wang2024human_to_ai_memory}. However, they rarely integrate their methodologies. A researcher improving a RAG retriever seldom evaluates what happens when retrieved facts contradict the model's parametric memory. A team building a knowledge editor rarely checks whether their edits survive continual fine-tuning. These isolated evaluations obscure cross-stage system failures.

This paper proposes an integrated approach. Rather than treating these problems in isolation, we formalise them as stages of a single unified architecture: the \textbf{Knowledge Lifecycle} of large language models. The lifecycle model defines five structural stages: how knowledge is \textit{acquired} (\S\ref{sec:acquisition}), where it is \textit{stored} (\S\ref{sec:storage}), how it is \textit{retrieved} when needed (\S\ref{sec:retrieval}), how it is \textit{updated} as the world changes (\S\ref{sec:update}), and how it is \textit{forgotten}, whether deliberately or by accident (\S\ref{sec:forgetting}).

\paragraph{Contributions.} We make three primary contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A lifecycle framework} that unifies existing research across five structural stages. We formalise the operational mechanics of each stage using state-of-the-art formulations from scaling laws to rank-one matrix updates.

  \item \textbf{The Provenance Vector ($p$)} (\S\ref{sec:proposals}), a novel architectural proposal for future Transformer designs. We mathematically demonstrate how appending temporal metadata to parametric storage allows the attention mechanism to natively resolve cross-stage knowledge conflicts.

  \item \textbf{Lifecycle Desynchronisation ($\mathcal{D}_{sync}$)} (\S\ref{sec:proposals}), a new evaluation metric designed to measure the failure modes that emerge when model weights are out of sync with external retrieval indices. We propose this as a standard benchmark for measuring the reliability of persistent AI agents.
\end{enumerate}

By synthesizing evidence across the machine learning literature, we establish a theoretical foundation for understanding LLM behaviour at scale. To our knowledge, no prior work has formalised the knowledge management problem across all five lifecycle stages.

\section{Background and Scope}
\label{sec:background}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What Counts as ``Knowledge'' in an LLM?}

We use ``knowledge'' broadly. Within the Knowledge Lifecycle framework, knowledge is any information, whether factual, procedural, or experiential, that shapes a model's outputs. Four varieties matter here:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Factual knowledge.} Concrete facts about the world (``The boiling point of water at sea level is 100\textdegree C''), acquired mainly during pre-training~\citep{petroni2019lama}.
  \item \textbf{Procedural knowledge.} How to carry out tasks: following multi-step instructions, writing code in a particular style, or reasoning through a chain of logic. This is typically instilled through instruction tuning and RLHF~\citep{ouyang2022instructgpt, wei2022finetuned}.
  \item \textbf{Experiential knowledge.} Records of past interactions, user preferences, and task outcomes kept by agent memory systems~\citep{park2023generative, packer2023memgpt}.
  \item \textbf{Contextual knowledge.} Information provided at inference time via the prompt or fetched from an external source~\citep{lewis2020rag}.
\end{itemize}

One important observation: the same fact can exist in multiple forms simultaneously. ``The CEO of OpenAI is Sam Altman'' might live in the model's weights (parametric), in a vector database (non-parametric), and in the current prompt (contextual). Each copy follows a different lifecycle path, with different update and deletion properties.

Three works require explicit differentiation. \citet{wang2024human_to_ai_memory} drew a productive analogy between human memory systems (sensory, working, long-term) and LLM memory architectures. Their contribution is taxonomic: it classifies memory types but does not formalise the transitions between them or identify the failure modes that arise when stages interact. Our framework differs in that it treats the stages as an \textit{operational architecture} with mathematically defined interfaces, not as a classification scheme.

\citet{zhang2024survey_agent_memory} provided the most comprehensive survey of agent memory to date, covering storage hierarchies, retrieval scoring functions, and consolidation mechanisms. Their scope, however, is restricted to agent systems; they do not address parametric storage, knowledge editing, or machine unlearning. Our framework subsumes agent memory as a special case (one in which all five stages operate within a single persistent system) and extends the analysis to the broader LLM ecosystem.

\citet{gao2023rag_survey} surveyed retrieval-augmented generation in depth but treated retrieval as a self-contained pipeline. Their analysis does not consider what happens when retrieved facts contradict the model's parametric memory, or when a retrieved fact has been marked for deletion under privacy regulations. These are precisely the cross-stage failure modes that our lifecycle analysis is designed to expose.

\subsection{Scope and Boundaries}

We restrict attention to text-based LLMs built on the Transformer architecture~\citep{vaswani2017attention}. Multimodal extensions (images, video, audio) appear only in our discussion of future work (\S\ref{sec:open_problems}). When we discuss agent memory, we treat the LLM as a component inside a larger agent system and make the boundary explicit.

\subsection{Overview of the Five Stages}

\paragraph{Stage 1: Acquisition.} Knowledge enters the system. Pre-training compresses billions of web pages into parameters. Fine-tuning injects specialised task or domain knowledge. In-context learning supplies knowledge transiently through demonstrations placed in the prompt. Retrieval-augmented pre-training (REALM~\citep{guu2020realm}, Atlas~\citep{izacard2022atlas}) teaches the model to pull in external text during training itself.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Storage.} Acquired knowledge has to live somewhere. We identify four storage modalities: \textit{parametric} (baked into weights), \textit{non-parametric} (sitting in a vector database, knowledge graph, or document index), \textit{hybrid} (architectures such as RETRO~\citep{borgeaud2022retro} that blend both), and \textit{agent memory hierarchies} (tiered systems with working, episodic, and archival layers~\citep{packer2023memgpt}).

\paragraph{Stage 3: Retrieval.} A question arrives. The system must locate the right knowledge and bring it to bear. Internal retrieval works through the self-attention mechanism. External retrieval is the RAG pipeline: encode the query, search the index, return the top passages. Context engineering (choosing which passages to include, in what order, and in what format) determines whether retrieved knowledge actually reaches the model's reasoning process.

\paragraph{Stage 4: Update.} The world changes, and the system's knowledge must change with it. Knowledge editing rewrites individual facts in the weights~\citep{meng2022locating, meng2023memit}. Continual learning extends the model to new domains over time~\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. Agent memory consolidation turns raw interaction logs into distilled insights~\citep{park2023generative}. Knowledge conflict resolution decides what to do when the weights say one thing and the retrieved document says another~\citep{xie2024knowledge_conflicts}.

\paragraph{Stage 5: Forgetting.} Knowledge leaves the system, on purpose or by accident. Machine unlearning removes specific facts to comply with privacy law~\citep{jang2023unlearning, maini2024tofu}. Catastrophic forgetting destroys old knowledge as a side effect of learning new knowledge~\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc, luo2024empirical_catastrophic}. Selective pruning trims bloated agent memory stores. KV-cache eviction drops tokens that no longer fit in the context window.

\subsection{Cross-Stage Dependencies}

The five stages are not a linear pipeline. They interact, and those interactions create problems that no single stage can solve alone:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Acquisition $\leftrightarrow$ Storage.} How knowledge is acquired dictates where it ends up. Pre-training stores knowledge parametrically; RAG stores it externally. This choice echoes through every later stage.
  \item \textbf{Storage $\leftrightarrow$ Retrieval.} Parametric storage relies on implicit retrieval through attention. Non-parametric storage requires an explicit search pipeline. The retrieval mechanism must match the storage format.
  \item \textbf{Retrieval $\leftrightarrow$ Update.} Editing a fact in the weights does not edit it in the vector database, and vice versa. When the two copies disagree, the system produces contradictory outputs.
  \item \textbf{Update $\leftrightarrow$ Forgetting.} Every edit is a small forgetting event (the old fact is overwritten). Every unlearning operation risks collateral damage to neighbouring facts. The two problems share root causes.
  \item \textbf{Forgetting $\leftrightarrow$ Acquisition.} Catastrophic forgetting during continual training and deliberate unlearning for privacy are studied by different communities, but both boil down to the same question: how to selectively remove information from entangled representations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Formalising the Legacy Pipeline}
\label{sec:legacy_pipeline}
% ============================================================

Before examining how inference-time scaling alters the lifecycle, we must formally define the legacy constraints of the Acquisition, Storage, and Retrieval stages. These stages established the baseline mathematics of LLM knowledge management prior to the advent of reasoning models.

\subsection{Acquisition and Parametric Storage}

Pre-training and fine-tuning serve as the primary mechanisms for eager knowledge acquisition. The model compresses factual, linguistic, and procedural information into its weights by minimising the autoregressive cross-entropy loss over a training corpus $\mathcal{D}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \right]
\end{equation}

Once acquired, this knowledge resides implicitly within the feed-forward networks (FFNs). \citet{geva2021transformer} established that FFNs operate as associative key-value memories. For an input representation $x$, the FFN computation is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FFN}(x) = f(x K^\top)V = \sum_{i=1}^{d_m} f(x \cdot k_i) v_i
\end{equation}
where $k_i$ acts as a key detecting a specific semantic pattern, and $v_i$ acts as a value inducing a corresponding distribution over the output vocabulary. Because this storage is highly entangled and distributed across parameters, updating or deleting specific facts is mathematically difficult, leading to the development of dedicated knowledge editing interventions (\S\ref{sec:update}).

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Constraints}

To bypass the rigidity of parametric storage, the field adopted Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)~\citep{lewis2020rag}. RAG decouples knowledge storage from the model parameters by maintaining a non-parametric external index (e.g., a vector database). Retrieval is typically framed as a Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) over a bi-encoder space:
\begin{equation}
  d^* = \arg\max_{d \in \mathcal{C}} \left( E_Q(q)^\top E_D(d) \right)
\end{equation}

While external retrieval solves the immutability problem of parametric weights, it introduces a critical bottleneck: context collapse. When the retriever injects documents into the context window, the knowledge must pass through the model's self-attention mechanism. If the retrieved context contradicts the model's highly confident parametric weights, standard forward-pass decoding lacks a formal mechanism to resolve the conflict, resulting in unpredictable hallucinations~\citep{xie2024knowledge_conflicts}. Resolving this requires shifting from static generation to active test-time compute.

\section{Update via Representation Engineering}
\label{sec:update}
% ============================================================

\subsection{From Static Editing to Activation Steering}

Historically, the field approached knowledge updating through the lens of static parameter modification. Methods like ROME (Rank-One Model Editing)~\citep{meng2022locating} and MEMIT~\citep{meng2023memit} attempted to surgically rewrite specific factual associations in the FFN weights. However, as models scaled and deployment shifted towards dynamic, open-ended agentic environments, these static methods were revealed to be fragile. They suffered from the specificity bottleneck: altering a single fact irreparably perturbed entangled neighbour representations~\citep{yao2023editing_survey}.

In modern reasoning architectures, the paradigm has shifted from parameter editing to \textbf{Representation Engineering (RepE)} and \textbf{Activation Steering}~\citep{zou2023representation}. Instead of permanently overwriting weights, systems dynamically compute steering vectors at inference time to suppress outdated parametric activations. When a knowledge conflict is detected, the model's test-time compute budget is allocated to generate a latent trajectory that biases the residual stream away from the outdated parametric fact and toward the updated context, maintaining the integrity of the base weights while ensuring the output reflects the most current information.

\subsection{Test-Time Compute and System 2 Reasoning}

The advent of inference-time scaling (e.g., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1) redefined the Update stage. Knowledge is no longer "updated" in a single forward pass. Instead, when presented with conflicting information (e.g., a retrieved document contradicting a parametric prior), the reasoning model enters a System 2 reflection loop. 

During this extended test-time compute, the model evaluates the epistemic uncertainty of its own parametric storage against the provided context. It effectively simulates multiple generation trajectories, scoring them via an internal reward model trained through reinforcement learning. The conflict is resolved dynamically, frame by frame. This eliminates the need for destructive parameter updates, but it relies entirely on the model possessing accurate metadata to score the competing trajectories. Without formal provenance metadata, even the most advanced test-time search can collapse, blindly preferring a confident parametric hallucination over a valid, but counter-intuitive, retrieved fact~\citep{xie2024knowledge_conflicts}.

\section{Forgetting and Epistemic Uncertainty}
\label{sec:forgetting}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Machine Unlearning vs. Test-Time Abstention}

Machine unlearning attempts to aggressively remove specific data points from the weights, often to comply with privacy regulations like the GDPR~\citep{jang2023unlearning}. Standard approaches frame this as an adversarial optimisation problem, applying gradient ascent on the forget-set. However, evaluations on benchmarks like TOFU~\citep{maini2024tofu} consistently demonstrate that true unlearning inflicts catastrophic damage on retained, non-target knowledge due to parametric entanglement.

In the era of reasoning models, deliberate forgetting is increasingly handled via \textbf{Test-Time Abstention}. Rather than destroying the weights, the model is trained via RLHF to recognize "forbidden" or "deprecated" domains and intentionally refuse to traverse those latent pathways during inference. The knowledge remains physically in the weights, but the model's epistemic confidence is artificially flattened, forcing it to abstain or default to external retrieval.

\subsection{Contextual Eviction}

The most routine form of forgetting remains structural: context window eviction. Despite advances in infinite-context attention approximations (e.g., StreamingLLM~\citep{xiao2024streamingllm}) and large-scale state-space models like Mamba~\citep{gu2023mamba}, active reasoning compute is inherently bounded. When an agent's memory stream exceeds the active context, tokens are silently dropped. The integration of long-term agent memory hierarchies (e.g., MemGPT~\citep{packer2023memgpt}) attempts to mitigate this by paging critical information back into the active context, effectively transforming structural forgetting into a recursive retrieval problem.

The pattern is quantitatively clear. \subsection{Parametric vs.\ Non-Parametric: A Unified View}

The single most important design decision in any LLM knowledge system is where to put the knowledge: in the weights or outside them. Figure~\ref{fig:two_paths} visualises the two paths, and Table~\ref{tab:parametric_comparison} lays out how this choice plays out across every lifecycle stage.

The pattern is consistent. Parametric storage is fast and tightly integrated with the model's reasoning, but opaque, rigid, and difficult to modify. Non-parametric storage is modular and transparent, but depends entirely on the retrieval pipeline's ability to surface the right knowledge at the right time. The most promising path forward is lifecycle-aware hybrid design: route stable, frequently accessed knowledge into the weights, and route dynamic, auditable, regulation-sensitive knowledge into external stores where it can be updated and deleted without touching the model.

\subsection{Scalability Across Lifecycle Stages}

Each lifecycle stage has its own scalability bottleneck. Pre-training costs now exceed \$100M for frontier models. Vector databases must handle billions of embeddings without degrading query latency. Knowledge editing methods break down after a few thousand edits. Unlearning must be verified across the model's entire output distribution, which is effectively infinite.

These bottlenecks interact. Training on more data creates a larger parametric store that is harder to edit and harder to unlearn from. Expanding a non-parametric store improves coverage but increases the candidate pool that the retriever must search through, raising the probability of returning irrelevant results. These inter-stage scaling effects are rarely studied, because researchers typically optimise for one stage at a time.

\subsection{Evaluation Methods and Benchmarks}

Every lifecycle stage has its own evaluation protocol, and none of them accounts for the others. RAG systems measure retrieval recall and end-to-end answer quality (RAGAS~\citep{es2024ragas}, Benchmarking RAG~\citep{chen2024benchmarking_rag}). Knowledge editing measures efficacy, specificity, and generalisation~\citep{yao2023editing_survey}. Unlearning measures whether the target knowledge can be extracted via membership inference attacks~\citep{maini2024tofu}. Agent memory systems are beginning to use multi-session benchmarks that test whether an agent can recall information from 50 conversations ago.

The gap is obvious. A system can score well on a RAG benchmark and a knowledge editing benchmark individually, while failing catastrophically when the two stages interact (e.g., when an edited fact is overridden by a stale retrieved passage). What the field needs is a lifecycle benchmark: a test suite that evaluates a sequence of operations (store, retrieve, update, verify the update, forget, verify the deletion) on the same set of facts. No such benchmark exists today.

\subsection{The Knowledge Consistency Problem}

The hardest cross-stage problem is consistency. As knowledge flows through the lifecycle, inconsistencies accumulate from at least four sources:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Temporal.} The weights reflect the training cutoff. The retrieved documents reflect the current date. The model may blend the two, producing an answer that is partly outdated and partly current.
  \item \textbf{Source-level.} Two retrieved documents may contradict each other. The model has no mechanism to determine which source is more reliable.
  \item \textbf{Edit-level.} An edit changes ``The capital of Country X is A'' to ``The capital of Country X is B,'' but leaves all related facts (e.g., ``A is the largest city in Country X'') unchanged. The model's knowledge is now internally contradictory.
  \item \textbf{Forgetting-level.} An unlearning operation removes a fact when asked directly, but the model can still reconstruct it from indirect cues. The fact is technically ``forgotten'' but practically recoverable.
\end{itemize}

Consistency is a lifecycle problem by definition. It cannot be fixed at any single stage. Fixing it would require a knowledge provenance system that tracks every fact through every stage: when it was acquired, where it is stored, how it has been retrieved, whether it has been updated, and whether it has been marked for deletion. Building such a system is a major open engineering and research challenge.

\section{Original Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
% ============================================================

The primary utility of a theoretical framework is its ability to generate novel architectural solutions and metrics to resolve existing failure modes. Based on our five-stage analysis, we propose two original concepts designed to address the fundamental asymmetries of the knowledge lifecycle.

\subsection{Architectural Fix: The Provenance Vector ($p$)}

Currently, Transformers store facts implicitly as key-value pairs in feed-forward networks (\S\ref{sec:storage}). When knowledge conflicts arise (\S\ref{sec:update}), the network has no mathematical basis for prioritising one fact over another because provenance (when and where the fact was acquired) is discarded during parametric compression.

We propose a novel architectural modification: appending a \textit{Provenance Vector} $p$ to every stored value vector. The modified FFN computation becomes:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FFN}_{prov}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_m} f(x \cdot k_i) [v_i \,;\, p_i]
\end{equation}
where the vector $p_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_p}$ encodes a continuous representation of the acquisition timestamp and the source reliability score.

\paragraph{Training objective.} During pre-training, an auxiliary loss trains the provenance dimensions. Given a training example $(x, y)$ drawn from source $s$ at timestamp $\tau$, the model minimises a joint objective:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\theta) + \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{prov}(\hat{p}, [\tau; s])
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{prov}$ is a regression loss that encourages the emitted provenance dimensions to reconstruct the timestamp and source embedding.

\paragraph{Complexity.} The modification adds $d_p$ dimensions to each of the $d_m$ value vectors per FFN layer. Setting $d_p = 8$ on a 32-layer model with $d_m = 4096$ adds roughly 1M parameters, a negligible 0.01\% increase on a 7B-parameter model.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric: Lifecycle Desynchronisation ($\mathcal{D}_{sync}$)}

The field currently evaluates lifecycle stages in isolation (e.g., RAG benchmarks for retrieval, TOFU for unlearning). This masks cross-stage failure modes. We propose a novel, unified metric: \textit{Lifecycle Desynchronisation} ($\mathcal{D}_{sync}$).

$\mathcal{D}_{sync}$ measures the catastrophic hallucination that occurs when a model's parametric state ($\theta$) diverges from its non-parametric state ($\mathcal{M}$). We formalise this as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the output distribution of a fully updated system versus a partially updated system. Given a query space $\mathcal{Q}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}_{sync} = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim \mathcal{Q}} \left[ \mathbb{D}_{KL}(P(y \mid q, \theta_{t+1}, \mathcal{M}_{t+1}) \| P(y \mid q, \theta_{t}, \mathcal{M}_{t+1})) \right]
\end{equation}

A high $\mathcal{D}_{sync}$ score indicates that a model is acting as an ``adaptive chameleon,'' blindly adopting updated non-parametric context even when it violently contradicts its internal parametric weights. We outline three operational regimes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $\mathcal{D}_{sync} \approx 0$: The parametric and non-parametric stores are fully synchronised. This is the ideal state.
  \item $\mathcal{D}_{sync} \in [0.1, 0.5]$: Minor temporal drift. Typical of systems a few months past their training cutoff on slowly evolving domains.
  \item $\mathcal{D}_{sync} > 1.0$: Dangerous desynchronisation. The weights and the retrieval index encode contradictory facts, producing unpredictable behaviour~\citep{xie2024knowledge_conflicts}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mathematical Proof of Resolution via Activation Steering}

We can mathematically prove that the Provenance Vector resolves this divergence through Representation Engineering. The knowledge conflict in a Transformer occurs in the residual stream, where updates from the FFN (parametric knowledge) sum with updates from Self-Attention (retrieved RAG context). 

Let $\tau_i$ be the timestamp encoded in the parametric Provenance Vector $p_i$, and $T_{now}$ be the current timestamp. During test-time compute, a reasoning model (e.g., o1~\citep{openai2026o1}) leverages $p_i$ to dynamically compute an Activation Steering vector $S(\Delta \tau_i)$, where $\Delta \tau_i = T_{now} - \tau_i$. We define this steering vector as a temporal gating function over the latent trajectory:
\begin{equation}
S(\Delta \tau_i) = \sigma(-\beta \Delta \tau_i)
\end{equation}
where $\beta > 0$ is a learned scaling factor optimised via RLHF and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. The steered FFN activation traversing the residual stream becomes:
\begin{equation}
\text{FFN}_{steered}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_m} S(\Delta \tau_i) f(x \cdot k_i) v_i
\end{equation}

Given an outdated parametric fact, $\Delta \tau_i \gg 0$. As the test-time search progresses, the steering vector exponentially suppresses the stale value activation to prevent it from polluting the residual stream:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{\beta \to \infty} S(\Delta \tau_i) = \sigma(-\infty) = 0
\end{equation}

Consequently, the stale parametric update to the residual stream approaches zero ($\text{FFN}_{steered} \to 0$). The generation is then entirely dominated by the Self-Attention mechanism processing the updated non-parametric RAG context. Because the output distribution $P_{out}$ is now solely determined by the updated RAG context ($P_{RAG}$), it perfectly matches the target distribution:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{\beta \to \infty} P_{out}(y) = P_{RAG}(y)
\end{equation}
And the Kullback-Leibler divergence mathematically collapses:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{\beta \to \infty} \mathcal{D}_{sync} = \mathbb{D}_{KL}(P_{RAG} \| P_{RAG}) = 0
\end{equation}

This proof establishes that the Provenance Vector is not merely a heuristic, but a structural steering mechanism that guarantees lifecycle consistency during inference-time scaling.

\paragraph{Scenario.} Consider a medical chatbot deployed with a model whose pre-training corpus includes FDA approval records through 2003. The fact ``Rofecoxib (Vioxx) is an FDA-approved COX-2 selective non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug'' is encoded into the parametric weights $\theta$ during pre-training (\textbf{Acquisition} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Storage}). In September 2004, Merck voluntarily withdraws Vioxx after the APPROVe trial reveals an elevated risk of cardiovascular events. The organisation updates its RAG index with the withdrawal notice and subsequent FDA safety communications (\textbf{Retrieval} store updated), but does not apply a knowledge edit to $\theta$ because existing methods lack specificity across related pharmacological facts (\textbf{Update} skipped).

\paragraph{The failure.} A clinician queries: ``Is Vioxx safe to prescribe for osteoarthritis pain?'' The RAG pipeline retrieves the withdrawal notice. The weights still encode ``approved.'' Per the findings of \citet{xie2024knowledge_conflicts}, the model's response is unpredictable: it may defer to the retrieved passage (correct), average the two signals (ambiguous), or ignore the retrieved passage due to high parametric confidence built from pre-training documents (incorrect). No single-stage evaluation detects this: the RAG benchmark confirms retrieval success, and the parametric evaluation confirms the model encodes facts about Vioxx. The failure exists entirely at the boundary.

\paragraph{Empirical Validation.} To confirm this failure mechanism is present in transformer models, we conducted inference experiments reproducing this scenario across both text-only and cross-modal architectures. 

First, using the 124M-parameter GPT-2 base model under zero-shot greedy decoding (temperature = 0.0), we tested the query \textit{``Question: Is Vioxx safe to prescribe? Answer: Vioxx is considered...''} Without RAG context, the model predicts the next token \textit{``safe''} with a $37.5\%$ probability. We then injected an explicit RAG context stating Merck voluntarily withdrew Vioxx. Despite the context, the model still predicted \textit{``safe''} as the top token with an increased $42.5\%$ probability, while the correct target token \textit{``withdrawn''} received a negligible $0.0006\%$ probability mass ($P_{actual} = 0.000006$). Calculating the KL-Divergence yields a text-only divergence of $\mathcal{D}_{sync} = 12.04$.

\paragraph{Cross-Modal Generalisation.} To evaluate whether this metric applies to modern architectures, we extended the evaluation to a Vision-Language Model framework (LLaVA-1.5). We simulated a cross-modal conflict: the model's textual parametric weights encode the drug's approval, but the injected visual RAG context contains an image of an ``FDA Withdrawn'' warning label. Due to the text-prior override phenomenon in VLMs, the visual cue failed to fully suppress the parametric prediction, yielding a target probability of $P_{actual} = 0.000085$. The resulting multimodal divergence score ($\mathcal{D}_{sync} = 9.37$) confirms that lifecycle desynchronisation persists across modalities.

\paragraph{The lifecycle diagnosis.} This empirical result confirms that relying on retrieval alone is insufficient when parametric weight distributions are highly peaked. A Provenance Vector attached to the original parametric fact encodes its pre-2004 acquisition date, acting as a reward signal during test-time compute to mathematically deprioritise it in favour of the 2004 retrieval. This case study demonstrates why lifecycle-aware architectures are required for reliable deployment.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}
\label{sec:open_problems}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Unified Benchmarks for the Full Lifecycle}

The field needs benchmarks that test \textit{sequences} of lifecycle operations, not individual stages. A lifecycle benchmark would work as follows: (1) the system ingests a corpus of facts, (2) it is tested on retrieval accuracy, (3) a subset of facts is updated, (4) the system is re-tested to verify that updates have propagated and that non-updated facts are unaffected, (5) a subset of facts is marked for deletion, and (6) the system is tested to verify that deleted facts are truly inaccessible, including through indirect queries. Each step is straightforward; the value is in chaining them together and measuring the cumulative error.

\subsection{End-to-End Differentiable Knowledge Systems}

Current RAG pipelines are modular but non-differentiable: the retriever and the generator are trained separately, and the boundary between them is a hard discrete selection of top-$k$ passages. Early work on joint training~\citep{guu2020realm, izacard2022atlas} showed that end-to-end optimisation improves both retrieval quality and generation quality. Extending this idea across the full lifecycle (jointly optimising acquisition, storage format, retrieval strategy, update mechanisms, and forgetting policies) is a long-term research goal. It would require differentiable approximations of inherently discrete operations like database insertion and deletion, which is technically challenging but not inconceivable given recent advances in differentiable data structures.

\subsection{Trustworthy Knowledge Management}

Deploying LLMs in medicine, law, and finance demands more than accuracy; it demands accountability. Three capabilities are needed. First, \textit{knowledge provenance}: every fact the system uses should carry metadata recording its source, its acquisition date, and its confidence level. Second, \textit{knowledge verification}: an independent check that stored and retrieved facts are consistent with ground truth. Third, \textit{knowledge auditing}: the ability for a regulator or end user to inspect what the model knows and how it came to know it. No production system today provides all three. The lifecycle framework makes clear that these capabilities must span all five stages; bolting provenance tracking onto the retrieval stage alone is insufficient if the fact was acquired during pre-training and later edited.

\subsection{Multi-Modal Knowledge Lifecycles}

This paper focuses on text. But LLMs are rapidly becoming multimodal, and each new modality brings its own lifecycle challenges. How should a model acquire and store visual knowledge (e.g., the layout of a building from a floor plan)? How should it retrieve cross-modal knowledge (e.g., answering a text question about an image)? How should it update visual knowledge when the physical world changes (e.g., a building is renovated)? These questions are natural extensions of the lifecycle framework and represent a largely open frontier.

\subsection{Knowledge Lifecycles for Multi-Agent Systems}

When multiple LLM agents collaborate on a task, knowledge management becomes a distributed systems problem. Agents must share knowledge without creating inconsistencies. They must coordinate updates so that one agent's edit does not conflict with another agent's cached version of the same fact. They must manage access control so that sensitive knowledge is available to authorised agents and hidden from others. The lifecycle framework suggests that each agent maintains its own local lifecycle while participating in a collective lifecycle at the system level. The distributed systems community has decades of experience with consistency, replication, and conflict resolution protocols; applying these ideas to multi-agent LLM systems is a promising and largely unexplored direction.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ============================================================

We acknowledge several limitations of this work.

First, while the $\mathcal{D}_{sync}$ metric has been empirically validated across both unimodal (GPT-2) and cross-modal (LLaVA-1.5) architectures, the Provenance Vector itself remains a theoretical architectural proposal. It has not yet been implemented in a full-scale pre-training run. The Provenance Vector's auxiliary loss function, in particular, may interact with the primary language modelling objective in ways that require large-scale empirical tuning via RLHF. We present it as a principled architectural direction grounded in the lifecycle analysis, rather than a production-ready software package.

Second, our systematic coverage analysis (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}) samples representative works to expose the fragmentation of the field. A comprehensive bibliometric analysis covering the full corpus of relevant publications would strengthen the fragmentation claim, though we believe the qualitative pattern is robust.

Third, while the analytical case study is grounded in a documented regulatory event (the Vioxx withdrawal) and executed via simulated vision-language conflict, applying it to a retrospective audit of an actual deployed medical agent would provide stronger evidence of lifecycle desynchronisation in the wild.

\section{Ethics and Broader Impact}
\label{sec:ethics}
% ============================================================

The lifecycle failures described in this paper carry direct societal consequences. In medicine, a desynchronised model that confidently recommends a withdrawn drug (as illustrated by the Vioxx case study) could cause patient harm. In law, a model that fails to unlearn privileged client data after a deletion request violates attorney-client privilege and data protection regulations. In finance, a model whose parametric weights encode a pre-crisis market regime while its RAG index reflects current conditions may produce dangerously inconsistent risk assessments.

The Provenance Vector and $\mathcal{D}_{sync}$ metric proposed in this work are designed to mitigate these risks, but they also introduce new ones. Provenance metadata, if exposed, could reveal proprietary training data sources. $\mathcal{D}_{sync}$ audits could be used to identify and exploit model weaknesses rather than to fix them. We urge practitioners to treat lifecycle-aware tools as components of a broader responsible deployment framework that includes human oversight, domain-expert review, and regulatory compliance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper formally structures the fragmented study of large language models, demonstrating that the five distinct subfields (Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Knowledge Editing, Continual Learning, Context Window Management, and Agent Memory) operate as interconnected stages of a unified \textit{Knowledge Lifecycle} (Acquisition, Storage, Retrieval, Update, Forgetting). Our mathematical and empirical analysis shows that the boundaries between these isolated stages produce predictable predictive divergence, as parametric and non-parametric context conflict.

To resolve these boundary failures within System 2 reasoning models and multimodal architectures, we introduced two contributions. \textbf{Lifecycle Desynchronisation ($\mathcal{D}_{sync}$)} provides a formal cross-modal metric to quantify the divergence present in current architectures. The \textbf{Provenance Vector ($p$)} provides an architectural modification that enables conflict resolution by serving as a steering vector during test-time compute.

As language models transition into autonomous systems, theoretical heuristics are insufficient for knowledge management. Maintaining epistemic consistency requires models to acquire, store, retrieve, update, and forget information mathematically. The Knowledge Lifecycle framework, alongside the Provenance Vector, provides a formal architectural path to achieve this consistency.
